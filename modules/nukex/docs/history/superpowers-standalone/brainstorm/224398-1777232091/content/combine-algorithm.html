Combine Algorithm — Mixed Broadband + Narrowband (LRGBSHO)

Pure-NB → calibrated palette. Pure-broadband → natural color. Mixed is the hard case — broadband gives natural color and detail, NB gives line-emission emphasis. Different combine algorithms produce different looks.

Inputs to the composer for an LRGBSHO image
After per-filter stacks complete, the composer sees:
Lbroadband luminance — best detail, full visible range R, G, Bbroadband chrominance — natural color across continuum Hanarrowband 656nm — strong in HII regions, dust emission OIIInarrowband 501nm — strong in planetary nebulae, ionization fronts SIInarrowband 672nm — supernova remnants, shocked gas

Three combine algorithms — pick one as the calibrated default

A

Lab/LCH composite — broadband L, NB-enhanced chrominance

1. Build natural-color RGB from broadband (L+RGB or OSC).
2. Convert to CIE-Lab.
3. Build NB-chrominance: Ha pushes a*-axis toward red, OIII pushes b*-axis toward blue (calibrated emission-line vectors). SII adds deep-red push.
4. Add NB chrominance into broadband Lab (a*, b* boost where NB signal is strong; clamped to gamut).
5. Convert Lab→RGB. L (detail) preserved from broadband.

Result: natural color base; HII regions get a calibrated red boost where Ha fires; OIII regions get a calibrated cyan-blue push. Greens only appear where the broadband actually has them (planet star colors, foreground stars). NB-only patches still respect the no-greens palette.

Why pick: preserves broadband detail (L stays untouched), color stays scientifically defensible. Standard for PixInsight LRGB+SHO workflows. Smooth handling of "what if NB signal is absent in this region" — falls back to natural color cleanly.

B

Continuum-subtracted emission overlay

1. Estimate continuum at Ha wavelength from broadband R (Ha is buried in the R bandpass — most of broadband R is continuum, not Ha).
2. Subtract continuum from Ha frame → "pure emission" Ha.
3. Same for OIII (continuum from interpolated G+B), SII (from R).
4. Pure emission gets added to broadband RGB at calibrated colors. Continuum-only regions remain natural-color.

Result: physically purest. Strong line emission stands out; continuum-only stars stay natural. Mathematically what professional H-alpha imaging does.

Why pick: highest scientific defensibility. Cleanest separation of line emission from continuum.

Cost: continuum estimation is fragile in low-SNR data; needs scale factors per camera/filter combo; can introduce subtraction artifacts on bright stars.

C

Soft additive (HaRGB-style)

1. Stretch broadband + NB independently.
2. NB added on top of matching broadband channel with a soft maximum: R_out = max(R_broadband, a*Ha) for some calibrated a. Same for OIII→B/G, SII→deep-R.
3. No Lab conversion; works directly in display RGB.

Result: NB-bright regions get punchier without darkening broadband-only areas. Familiar look — close to PixelMath HaRGB recipe.

Why pick: simplest math, easy to debug, easy to predict. No color-space conversion bugs.

Cost: not strictly calibrated (the "a" weights are tuned by feel); can produce hue shifts in magenta when Ha + R both saturate; doesn't honor the calibrated-palette goal as cleanly.

D

A as default, with B as an opt-in advanced mode

Ship A (Lab/LCH) as the calibrated default — Auto picks it, fits the simple-UI rule. Expose B (continuum subtraction) as a separately selectable mode for users who want the maximum physical purity and have the SNR for it.

Why pick: covers both scientific levels in one design.

Cost: two combine paths to write, test, and maintain. Phase 8 ratings would need to know which path is active.
